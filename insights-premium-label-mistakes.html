Insights / 04 – PRODUCTION

Eight ways premium labels go wrong at press

Foil, emboss and varnish are what make a premium pack feel premium. They are also where the money gets lost. These are the mistakes that recur most, and what to do instead.

CARL CURCURUTO · PROOF CREATIVE

01

Butting foil against varnish with no trap

Registration on a moving web is never perfect. Where a foil edge is drawn to meet a varnish edge exactly, the press will either leave a hairline of bare stock or overlap the two, and both read as a dirty edge on a pack people are holding at arm’s length.

INSTEAD

Specify a deliberate trap, and decide which of the two processes is dominant, rather than leaving the press to resolve it.

02

Registering emboss to foil on fine detail

Foil and emboss hitting the same small element is the tightest tolerance on the job. On fine type or thin rules it will drift, and once it drifts the pack looks like a mistake rather than a decision.

INSTEAD

Emboss to a shape that can tolerate a little movement, or take the fine work in foil alone.

03

Linework below the minimum the process can hold

Hairlines and fine engraving look exquisite on screen at 400 per cent. On press they fill in, break up or vanish entirely, and it depends on the process, the substrate and the run.

INSTEAD

Get the minimum line weight in writing from the printer, then put it in the brief to the illustrator before they draw, not in a list of corrections afterwards.

04

Signing off colour from a digital proof on a special substrate

Uncoated, textured and metallised stocks all shift colour, sometimes considerably. A digital proof on proofing stock tells you very little about what an ink will do on the material you have actually chosen.

INSTEAD

Wet proof on the real stock where the pack warrants it, and build the time for it into the schedule from the start.

05

Expecting a foil to match a printed colour

Foils are not Pantones. They are reflective, they pick up their surroundings, and they change completely with the angle you hold them at. A foil chosen to match a printed gold on screen will rarely match it in the hand.

INSTEAD

Choose the foil physically, from samples, under the light the pack will actually be seen in.

06

Stacking too many finishes on the same area

Every process added is another registration tolerance to hold, another pass through a machine, and another opportunity to spoil the sheet. Premium does not mean all of them at once, and cost rises faster than perceived value.

INSTEAD

Decide which single finish carries the idea, and let the others support it or go.

07

Forgetting what finishing does to what is underneath

A soft-touch laminate will dull a foil and pull colour down. A gloss varnish will deepen it. The pack you approved flat is not the pack that comes back finished.

INSTEAD

Judge from a finished sample with the actual laminate and varnish applied, not from a flat proof.

08

Approving the proof under the wrong light

A pack approved under studio daylight will be bought in a warm-lit bar, a bright supermarket aisle, or a dim bottle shop. Metallics and deep colours in particular behave very differently in each.

INSTEAD

Look at the proof under the light the pack will live in, as well as under standardised viewing conditions.

The thread running through all eight

None of these are exotic failures. They are all the same failure at different points: a decision that costs nothing while the design can still move, and costs a reprint once it cannot. Trap widths, minimum line weights, achievable registration and foil behaviour are all knowable in advance. They are only expensive when nobody asks.

Which is why the printer belongs in the conversation before the design is signed off, not after. They are not there to say no. They are there to tell you what they can hold, so the design can be built around it.
